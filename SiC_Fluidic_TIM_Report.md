## Abstract

Silicon carbide (SiC) MOSFET power modules are rapidly displacing silicon IGBTs in EV traction inverters, railway drives, and defence power converters owing to their higher switching frequency, elevated junction temperature capability (175–200 °C), and superior power density. However, the thermal path—not the semiconductor—remains the actual performance-limiting factor. The combination of extreme CTE mismatch between the SiC die (~4.0 ppm/K) and the copper baseplate (~17 ppm/K), thermomechanical fatigue at the die-attach and substrate-to-baseplate interfaces, and heat flux densities approaching 500 W/cm² together constrain device lifetime and derate achievable power, and existing thermal interface materials (TIMs) are not designed for this combination of high-temperature operation, severe CTE mismatch, and concentrated heat flux.

This project scopes a **fluidic-embedded TIM**: a compliant interface layer carrying internal microfluidic coolant channels, intended to sit between a packaged SiC power module and its heat sink in place of the standard TIM-plus-separate-coldplate stack. The work is structured in two phases. **Phase One** uses coupled conjugate heat transfer simulation to quantify a previously unresolved tradeoff between bond-line thickness and channel geometry, benchmarking the concept against the conventional architecture under SiC-relevant thermal boundary conditions. **Phase Two**, contingent on securing microfabrication access, prototypes and tests the most promising geometry.

This targets a deliberately narrow, currently uncontested niche: well-funded microfluidic cooling companies (e.g., Corintis, Jetcool) target large-die, uniform-flux data centre silicon, not the small-die, CTE-mismatch-limited, high-temperature, transient-heavy thermal profile of SiC power modules used in automotive and defence applications. The outcome has a direct path into India's iDEX DRISHTI defence innovation funding track and aligns with the national push for indigenous SiC power electronics capability.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The SiC Thermal Paradox

4H-SiC possesses intrinsically excellent thermal properties—a bulk thermal conductivity of 370–490 W/m·K and a wide bandgap enabling junction temperatures theoretically exceeding 600 °C. Yet in practice, the *packaging* is the bottleneck. Commercial SiC MOSFETs are rated to $T_{j,\text{max}}$ of 175–200 °C, a limit set not by the semiconductor but by the reliability of solder joints, substrate metallisations, wire bonds, and encapsulant materials that degrade under the extreme thermomechanical stress generated by high-temperature operation.

### 1.2 Why SiC Is Thermally Harder Than GaN-on-Si

While GaN-on-Si devices face a substrate thermal conductivity bottleneck (silicon's 150 W/m·K limits lateral heat spreading), SiC devices face a fundamentally different and in many ways more severe set of challenges:

| Parameter | GaN-on-Si | SiC (4H-SiC) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Die thermal conductivity | ~150 W/m·K (Si substrate) | 370–490 W/m·K |
| $T_{j,\text{max}}$ (practical) | 150 °C | 175–200 °C |
| CTE of die | ~2.6 ppm/K (Si) | ~4.0–5.0 ppm/K |
| CTE of Cu baseplate | ~17 ppm/K | ~17 ppm/K |
| CTE mismatch (die to Cu) | ~14 ppm/K | ~12–13 ppm/K |
| $\Delta T$ in thermal cycling | ~130 K | ~175 K |
| Thermomechanical stress ($\propto \Delta\alpha \cdot \Delta T$) | Moderate | **Severe** |
| Typical device heat flux | 100–400 W/cm² | 200–500 W/cm² |
| Die size (for given power) | Larger (limited by GaN-on-Si cost) | **Smaller** (higher $/mm²) |
| Primary failure mode | Substrate thermal resistance | **Die-attach fatigue & TIM degradation** |

The critical difference: SiC modules operate at higher temperatures with larger thermal swings, generating more severe thermomechanical stress at every interface in the packaging stack. The smaller die size for a given power rating further concentrates heat flux. And unlike GaN-on-Si, where the substrate can be co-designed with embedded cooling channels, SiC power modules are purchased as packaged units by system integrators who cannot modify the die or substrate.

### 1.3 The TIM Problem in SiC Modules

The thermal interface material between a SiC power module's baseplate and the system heat sink must simultaneously:

1. **Conduct heat** from concentrated, high-flux sources (200–500 W/cm²) with minimal thermal resistance
2. **Accommodate CTE mismatch** between the module baseplate (Cu, ~17 ppm/K) and the heat sink (typically Al, ~23 ppm/K, or Cu) through mechanical compliance
3. **Survive high-temperature operation** (baseplate temperatures routinely reaching 125 °C) without pump-out, dry-out, or chemical degradation
4. **Endure aggressive thermal cycling** ($\Delta T$ of 100–175 K, thousands of cycles over module lifetime)

No existing TIM formulation simultaneously satisfies all four requirements. Thermal greases pump out. Phase-change materials dry out. Metallic TIMs (indium, liquid metal) are expensive and raise galvanic corrosion concerns. Sintered interfaces are rigid and cannot accommodate post-assembly CTE mismatch.

## 2. Literature Survey

SiC power module thermal management sits at the intersection of three separately mature but not yet combined research threads: (i) advanced packaging and substrate-level cooling, (ii) microchannel and embedded cooling architectures, and (iii) thermal interface material reliability. The following review summarises the key sources establishing all three threads and the gap between them.

### 2.1 Embedded and Substrate-Level Microchannel Cooling

**van Erp, R., Kampitsis, G., Nela, L., et al. (2020) — Embedded Microchannel Cooling for High Power-Density GaN-on-Si Power Integrated Circuits (IEEE)**

Demonstrated cooling directly through microchannels etched into the backside of the Si substrate beneath GaN-on-Si power ICs, turning the substrate itself into the heat sink and removing the TIM from the thermal path. A proof-of-concept full-wave bridge rectifier cooled a device-level heat flux of 417 W/cm² using only 60 mW of pumping power.

> **Contribution:** Established substrate-embedded microchannel cooling as a mature, published approach that eliminates the TIM entirely, and is the origin research line behind the company Corintis. However, this approach requires wafer-level co-design and is not applicable to packaged SiC modules purchased off the shelf.

**van Erp, R., et al. (2020) — Bringing the Heat Sink Closer to the Heat: Evaluating Die-Embedded Microchannel Cooling of GaN-on-Si Power Devices (THERMINIC)**

Presented cooling strategies with microchannels embedded directly inside semiconductor dies, extracting higher heat fluxes while substantially reducing pumping power compared to remote heat sinks.

> **Contribution:** Confirmed die-level channel embedding as a further extension of the substrate-embedded approach. Neither this work nor the preceding one has been demonstrated for SiC substrates, where the much higher hardness and chemical inertness of SiC make backside etching significantly more challenging.

**Thermal Property Evaluation of a 2.5D Integration Method with Device Level Microchannel Direct Cooling for a High-Power GaN HEMT Device (PMC, 2022)**

Used a high-resistivity silicon interposer embedded with four-layer microchannels beneath a GaN HEMT device, reaching a maximum surface temperature of 93.8 °C at a gate-finger heat dissipation density of 32 kW/cm² with DI water coolant.

> **Contribution:** Quantified how close coolant must get to the heat source to be effective at extreme heat flux densities, directly motivating the bond-line-thickness question this project investigates for SiC-relevant conditions.

### 2.2 SiC-Specific Thermal Management and Packaging

**Thermal Management of Wide-Bandgap Power Semiconductors: Strategies and Challenges in SiC and GaN Power Devices (MDPI Electronics, 2025)**

Reviewed direct liquid cooling, jet impingement, phase-change, and embedded microchannel strategies for SiC and GaN devices, identifying interface resistance as a primary reliability bottleneck in both SiC and GaN module architectures.

> **Contribution:** Confirmed that interfacial thermal resistance—not bulk die or substrate conduction—is the recognised bottleneck in current wide-bandgap device literature, and that this bottleneck is especially acute in SiC modules operating at higher temperatures.

**Double-Sided Cooling Architectures for SiC Power Modules (multiple sources, 2024–2025)**

Recent work from multiple groups has demonstrated double-sided cooling (DSC) structures for SiC modules, providing two parallel heat dissipation paths and reducing junction-to-case thermal resistance ($R_{\text{th,jc}}$) by 30% or more. These designs use 3D-stacked DBC substrates with copper spacers or clips replacing wire bonds.

> **Contribution:** DSC addresses the problem from the packaging side but still requires a TIM at the module-to-heatsink interface. The fluidic-embedded TIM concept is complementary to, not competitive with, DSC—it improves the external thermal interface rather than the internal module stack.

**Silver Sintering Die-Attach for SiC Power Modules: Reliability Under Thermal Cycling (multiple sources, 2024–2025)**

Research validates the superior thermal fatigue resistance of sintered silver versus traditional solder for SiC die-attach, with some designs showing no cracking after 1,000 thermal cycles at $-50$ °C to $+250$ °C. However, sintered interfaces are rigid and concentrate stress at the substrate-to-baseplate layer.

> **Contribution:** Established that while die-attach reliability is improving through advanced materials (Ag sintering), this shifts the weakest thermal link outward—to the TIM between baseplate and heat sink—precisely the interface this project targets.

### 2.3 TIM Reliability and Advanced TIM Concepts

**Nagrani, P. P., and Marconnet, A. M. (2026) — A New Accelerated Method to Characterize Degradation of Thermal Interface Materials (ASME)**

Documented that thermal greases degrade over time through pump-out and dry-out under repeated thermal cycling, and that traditional reliability testing methods require long testing periods, motivating a new accelerated mechanical-cycling characterisation method.

> **Contribution:** Established that TIM pump-out and dry-out remain an open, unstandardised reliability problem industry-wide. This degradation is *accelerated* in SiC applications due to the higher operating temperatures and wider thermal excursions.

**Cheng, R., Wang, Q., Wang, Z., et al. (2025) — Liquid-infused nanostructured composite as a high-performance thermal interface material for effective cooling (Nature Communications)**

Reported a liquid-metal nanostructured composite (LINC) using a copper nanowire scaffold whose hydrophilicity and friction trap the infused liquid, showing no observable degradation over more than 2,600 power cycles and thermal resistance below 1 mm²·K/W.

> **Contribution:** Demonstrated a concrete composite-design strategy for pump-out resistance, which this project draws on for the fluidic-embedded TIM's mechanical design. However, LINC is a passive TIM—it does not carry active coolant flow.

**Elastic Liquid Metal–Polymer TIMs (NSF-funded research, 2024–2025)**

Startups and research labs are creating soft, stretchable polymers embedded with microscale droplets of liquid metal, combining the high thermal conductivity of metals with the mechanical reliability of polymers.

> **Contribution:** Confirmed industry interest in hybrid TIM architectures that combine fluidic and structural elements, validating the general design philosophy of this project.

### 2.4 Microfluidic Cooling Industry Landscape

**Corintis Series A Funding and Microsoft Microfluidic Cooling Collaboration (Data Center Dynamics, 2025)**

Reported that Corintis, founded out of the EPFL research line, raised a $24 million Series A (total funding $33.4 million) to scale microfluidic cooling systems co-designed for large-die data centre AI chips, having already shipped over 10,000 systems.

> **Contribution:** Established that current, well-capitalised microfluidic cooling players are targeting large, uniform-flux data centre silicon, not small-die, CTE-mismatch-limited SiC power modules. This market gap is the niche this project addresses.

**Modular Microchannel Thermal Solutions for Integrated Circuit Devices (US Patent)**

Described a thermal management stack in which a microfluidic device sits on top of a separate thermal interface material layer between the IC chip and the microfluidic device—i.e., the TIM and the coolant channels remain two distinct, stacked components.

> **Contribution:** Confirmed that the specific concept of a single sealed fluidic TIM material, rather than a TIM-plus-separate-microfluidic-device stack, has not been claimed as prior art in this search.

### 2.5 India's SiC Ecosystem and Defence Context

**DRDO Indigenous SiC Wafer and GaN HEMT Development (PIB, 2025)**

DRDO successfully developed indigenous processes for manufacturing 4-inch SiC wafers and GaN HEMTs capable of handling power levels up to 150 W, critical for next-generation radars, electronic warfare systems, and missile platforms.

> **Contribution:** Established that India is actively building indigenous SiC capability for defence applications, creating a direct demand signal for thermal management solutions that work with SiC power modules.

**iDEX Defence Innovation Framework**

iDEX continues to act as a link between startups, MSMEs, and the military, with projects involving wide-bandgap semiconductors prioritised due to their superior thermal resistance and efficiency in harsh military environments.

> **Contribution:** Confirmed a viable funding and adoption pathway for SiC thermal management innovations targeting defence applications in India.

### 2.6 Fabrication Access Routes

**Indian Nanoelectronics Users Programme (INUP-i2i)**

A MeitY-funded national nanofabrication access programme providing training and fabrication access at six premier institutions: **IISc Bangalore, IIT Bombay, IIT Delhi, IIT Guwahati, IIT Kharagpur, and IIT Madras**. Access requires registration on the INUP-i2i portal, participation in familiarisation workshops and hands-on training, and competitive submission of a research project proposal.

> **Contribution:** Identified the primary fabrication access route for the Phase Two synthesis stage of this project. The multi-institutional structure provides several independent options; specific institutional choice should be driven by equipment availability, microfluidics capability, and proposal competitiveness, not pre-existing lab affiliations.

**National Nanofabrication Centre (NNFC), CeNSE, IISc Bangalore**

Houses 14,000 sq. ft. of cleanroom space (Class 100/1000) for lithography, deposition, and etching, accessible through the INUP-i2i programme or direct registration via the CeNSE user portal. Requires safety training, project approval, and biometric access.

**IIT Bombay Nanofabrication Facility (IITBNF)**

Offers complementary fabrication capabilities accessible through the same INUP-i2i framework.

> **Note on Fabrication Strategy:** The choice of fabrication facility should be determined by (a) alignment of available equipment with the specific microfluidic channel geometries and materials required, (b) availability of slots and turnaround time, and (c) proposal merit. No single institution is assumed or preferred a priori.

## 3. Literature Gap

The reviewed literature provides extensive, mature coverage of three separate threads:

1. **Substrate- and die-embedded microchannel cooling** for wide-bandgap devices (the EPFL/Corintis line)
2. **Advanced SiC module packaging** including double-sided cooling, silver sintering, and CTE-matched substrates
3. **TIM reliability** including pump-out/dry-out characterisation and emerging composite designs (LINC, LM-polymer hybrids)

However:

- The **embedded-cooling literature** removes the TIM entirely by co-designing channels into the die or substrate—an approach requiring wafer-level access unavailable to integrators buying discrete SiC power modules off the shelf.
- The **advanced packaging literature** improves internal module thermal resistance but still requires a high-performance, reliable TIM at the module-to-heatsink interface.
- The **TIM reliability literature** and recent composite designs address pump-out resistance but do not carry active coolant flow within the TIM itself.
- The **microfluidic industry** targets large-die, uniform-flux data centre workloads, not the small-die, high-temperature, CTE-mismatch-limited, transient-heavy profile of SiC power modules.

**No source reviewed demonstrates a single, sealed fluidic-channel TIM material combining both functions, nor quantifies the bond-line-thickness-versus-channel-geometry tradeoff that determines whether such a material could outperform the standard TIM-plus-separate-coldplate stack under SiC-relevant operating conditions.** This project addresses that specific, narrow gap.

## 4. Objectives

1. **Quantify the bond-line-thickness-versus-channel-geometry tradeoff** for a fluidic-embedded TIM using coupled conjugate heat transfer simulation, under SiC module heat flux boundary conditions (200–500 W/cm²) and operating temperatures ($T_\text{baseplate}$ up to 125 °C) drawn from published values and manufacturer datasheets.

2. **Benchmark the fluidic-embedded TIM concept** against the standard architecture (conventional TIM plus a separate solid coldplate with equivalent channel geometry) to identify whether, and under what parameter regime, a real thermal resistance advantage exists.

3. **Map the compliance-versus-rigidity design space** to determine whether a single material can satisfy both the mechanical conformity a TIM needs (to accommodate CTE mismatch between Cu baseplate and Al/Cu heat sink) and the sealing rigidity a fluidic channel needs, or whether a layered/composite approach is required. This is especially critical for SiC given the higher operating temperatures that soften polymeric matrices.

4. **Evaluate high-temperature material candidates** for the fluidic-embedded TIM matrix, considering that SiC baseplate temperatures (up to 125 °C) preclude many polymer-based TIM materials that are adequate for lower-temperature silicon or GaN applications.

5. **Identify the most promising candidate geometry and material combination** from the Phase One design map for potential physical prototyping.

6. **Investigate realistic microfabrication access routes** through the INUP-i2i programme and direct facility applications at nationally accessible cleanrooms (IISc CeNSE/NNFC, IITBNF, or others), selecting the facility best matched to the required fabrication processes on merit.

7. **If fabrication access is secured**, prototype and test a sealed fluidic-embedded TIM sample against the Phase One simulation predictions.

8. **Document findings**, including negative or inconclusive results, as the basis for a possible iDEX DRISHTI submission targeting SiC power module thermal reliability for defence power electronics.

## 5. Scope

This project covers the design-space simulation and, contingent on fabrication access, physical prototyping of a fluidic-embedded thermal interface material for **SiC power modules**. It is explicitly scoped as a two-phase effort:

- **Phase One (Simulation)** is fully achievable with current computational resources and is treated as a complete, standalone contribution even if Phase Two does not proceed.
- **Phase Two (Synthesis)** is a stretch goal dependent on securing cleanroom microfabrication access and is not scheduled or promised until that access is confirmed.

### In Scope

- Coupled conjugate heat transfer simulation of microfluidic channel geometries embedded in a compliant or composite TIM matrix
- Parameter sweeps across channel width (10–200 µm), pitch (50–500 µm), and bond-line thickness (50–500 µm)
- Thermal boundary conditions derived from published SiC MOSFET module data: heat flux 200–500 W/cm², $T_{j,\text{max}}$ = 175–200 °C, $T_\text{baseplate}$ up to 125 °C
- Mechanical compliance assessment via analytical CTE mismatch stress estimation
- Benchmarking against the standard TIM + coldplate stack
- Investigation of high-temperature-tolerant matrix materials (silicone elastomers, PDMS alternatives, fluoroelastomers, metal-matrix composites)

### Out of Scope

- Replication of or competition with substrate- or die-embedded microchannel cooling (the EPFL/Corintis line), which requires wafer-level co-design unavailable for off-the-shelf SiC modules
- Modification of the internal SiC module packaging (die-attach, substrate, wire bonds)
- Full reliability qualification (JEDEC/AQG 324 power cycling) — this is a design-space exploration, not a qualification programme
- Electrical characterisation of SiC devices under the proposed cooling scheme

### Target Application

Retrofit-style thermal management of **existing, packaged SiC power modules** used in EV traction inverters and defence power electronics, where the device and substrate cannot be redesigned by the system integrator.

## 6. Methodology

### 6.1 Phase One: Simulation

#### 6.1.1 Geometry and Domain

The simulation domain models a representative cross-section of the thermal stack:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
│         SiC MOSFET Die              │  ← Heat source (q" = 200–500 W/cm²)
├─────────────────────────────────────┤
│    Die-attach (Ag sinter, 20 µm)    │
├─────────────────────────────────────┤
│   DBC Substrate (AlN + Cu)          │
├─────────────────────────────────────┤
│   Baseplate-attach (solder)         │
├─────────────────────────────────────┤
│       Cu Baseplate                  │  ← T_baseplate up to 125 °C
╞═════════════════════════════════════╡
│                                     │
│   FLUIDIC-EMBEDDED TIM              │  ← THIS PROJECT'S FOCUS
│   ┌───┐   ┌───┐   ┌───┐   ┌───┐   │
│   │ ≋ │   │ ≋ │   │ ≋ │   │ ≋ │   │  ← Microfluidic channels
│   └───┘   └───┘   └───┘   └───┘   │
│                                     │
╞═════════════════════════════════════╡
│      Heat Sink (Al or Cu)           │  ← Convective BC or T_fixed
└─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### 6.1.2 Simulation Approach

**Software:** COMSOL Multiphysics (if institutionally licensed) or OpenFOAM (open-source fallback)

**Physics:**
- Conjugate heat transfer: solid conduction + laminar fluid flow in microchannels
- Steady-state and transient analysis (switching-frequency thermal transients)
- Parametric sweep:
  - Channel width: 10, 25, 50, 100, 200 µm
  - Channel pitch: 50, 100, 200, 300, 500 µm
  - Bond-line thickness (total TIM height): 50, 100, 200, 300, 500 µm
  - Coolant flow rate: 10–500 mL/min (DI water)
  - Matrix material: silicone elastomer ($k$ ≈ 0.2 W/m·K), filled silicone ($k$ ≈ 3–5 W/m·K), metal-loaded composite ($k$ ≈ 10–30 W/m·K)

**Boundary Conditions:**
- Top surface: uniform or gate-finger-patterned heat flux (200–500 W/cm²)
- Bottom surface: convective BC ($h$ = 1,000–10,000 W/m²·K) or fixed temperature
- Coolant inlet: $T_\text{in}$ = 25 °C (or 65 °C for automotive glycol-water)
- Periodic BC on lateral faces

**Outputs:**
- Junction-to-coolant thermal resistance $R_{\text{th,j-c}}$ as a function of geometry
- Maximum temperature rise $\Delta T_\text{max}$
- Pressure drop $\Delta P$ and required pumping power
- Temperature uniformity (max–min spread across die footprint)

#### 6.1.3 Benchmarking

The fluidic-embedded TIM is benchmarked against:
1. **Baseline 1:** Thermal grease (BLT = 50 µm, $k$ = 5 W/m·K) + separate microchannel coldplate
2. **Baseline 2:** Phase-change TIM (BLT = 25 µm, $k$ = 3 W/m·K) + separate microchannel coldplate
3. **Baseline 3:** Indium foil TIM (BLT = 75 µm, $k$ = 80 W/m·K) + separate microchannel coldplate

All baselines use the same total thermal-path length and same coolant conditions.

#### 6.1.4 Mechanical Compliance Assessment

Analytical estimation of thermomechanical stress at the TIM interfaces:

$$\sigma_\text{shear} \approx \frac{(\alpha_\text{baseplate} - \alpha_\text{heatsink}) \cdot \Delta T \cdot G_\text{TIM} \cdot L}{t_\text{TIM}}$$

where $G_\text{TIM}$ is the shear modulus of the TIM matrix, $L$ is the half-length of the bonded area, and $t_\text{TIM}$ is the bond-line thickness. The fluidic channels reduce the effective shear modulus (increasing compliance) but also introduce stress concentrators at channel walls.

### 6.2 Phase Two: Synthesis (Contingent)

If fabrication access is secured through the INUP-i2i programme or direct facility application:

1. **Mould fabrication** via soft lithography (SU-8 master → PDMS or fluoroelastomer replica) or direct laser machining
2. **Channel sealing** via plasma bonding or adhesive lamination
3. **Thermal testing** via ASTM D5470-derived steady-state method with calibrated heat source
4. **Comparison** of measured $R_\text{th}$ and $\Delta P$ against Phase One predictions

## 7. Material Considerations for SiC Operating Conditions

SiC modules operate at significantly higher temperatures than silicon-based devices, imposing additional constraints on the TIM matrix material:

| Material | $k$ (W/m·K) | $T_\text{max}$ (°C) | Shear Modulus | SiC Suitability |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---|
| PDMS (Sylgard 184) | 0.15–0.27 | 200 | Very low | Marginal: adequate $T_\text{max}$ but very low $k$ |
| Filled silicone elastomer | 1–5 | 200–250 | Low | **Good**: acceptable $k$ and $T$, high compliance |
| Fluoroelastomer (Viton) | 0.2–0.3 | 250 | Low–moderate | Good: high $T_\text{max}$, chemical inertness |
| LTCC (low-temp co-fired ceramic) | 2–5 | >500 | High | Rigid: good $T$ but no compliance |
| Metal-filled polymer composite | 5–30 | 200+ | Moderate | **Promising**: tuneable $k$ and compliance |
| Copper microchannel + compliant gasket | 400 (Cu) | >500 | Mixed | Hybrid approach: rigid channels + compliant seals |

The **filled silicone elastomer** and **metal-filled polymer composite** routes are the primary candidates for the compliant TIM matrix. A hybrid approach using rigid metal or ceramic microchannels with compliant gaskets at the bonding interfaces is also evaluated as a design variant.

## 10. Risk Assessment

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---|
| Simulation shows no thermal advantage over standard TIM + coldplate | Medium | High | A null result is itself a valuable, publishable finding that bounds the design space. |
| Bond-line thickness required for channels exceeds useful TIM thickness | Medium | High | Parameter sweep explicitly maps this tradeoff; pivot to thinner, higher-$k$ matrix materials. |
| Matrix material softens or degrades at SiC operating temperatures | Medium | Medium | Evaluate fluoroelastomers and metal-filled composites rated to >200 °C. |
| Fabrication access not secured for Phase Two | Medium | Low | Phase One is a standalone contribution; Phase Two is explicitly a stretch goal. |
| Channel sealing fails under thermal cycling | Low–Medium | High | Use bonding methods validated for MEMS (plasma bonding, thermocompression). |
| Coolant leakage into electrical interfaces | Low | Critical | Design sealed, self-contained channels with leak detection provisions. |

## 11. Expected Contributions

1. **First published design map** of the bond-line-thickness-versus-channel-geometry tradeoff for a fluidic-embedded TIM under SiC-relevant thermal conditions.

2. **Quantitative benchmark** of the fluidic-embedded TIM concept against the conventional TIM + coldplate stack, identifying the parameter regime (if any) where a net thermal resistance advantage exists.

3. **Material selection framework** for high-temperature-compatible TIM matrices carrying embedded microfluidic channels, accounting for both thermal and mechanical requirements specific to SiC power modules.

4. **Identification of whether the compliance-versus-rigidity problem can be solved** within a single material or requires a composite/hybrid approach—a result directly useful for TIM and MEMS communities.

5. **Documentation of negative or inconclusive results** as a defensible, evidence-based basis for deciding whether to pursue physical prototyping and, subsequently, an iDEX DRISHTI submission targeting SiC power module thermal reliability for defence applications.
